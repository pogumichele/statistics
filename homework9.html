Interpretations & The Axiomatic Foundation

1. Resolving Inconsistencies

Before Kolmogorov, probability was fragmented. The axiomatic approach provides a single grammatical structure for the language of chance.

Classical

  • Origin: Laplace (Games of chance).
  • Idea: $N(A) / N(\Omega)$.
  • Flaw: Circular definition (requires "equiprobable" outcomes) & fails for infinite spaces.

Frequentist

  • Origin: Von Mises (Data).
  • Idea: Limit of relative frequency as $n \to \infty$.
  • Flaw: Cannot assign probability to single, non-repeatable events (e.g., "nuclear war probability").

Subjectivist

  • Origin: De Finetti/Savage.
  • Idea: Degree of belief / Betting odds.
  • Flaw: Lacks objectivity; purely internal consistency.

Axiomatic (Solution)

  • Origin: Kolmogorov (1933).
  • Idea: Defines rules $P(\cdot)$ must follow, ignoring "what" probability *is*.
  • Result: Unifies all above as valid models within a consistent measure-theoretic framework.

2. Probability as Measure Theory

Probability is Measure Theory with total mass = 1. The triplet $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ is the foundation.

Space: $\Omega$ (Set of outcomes)
Structure: $\mathcal{F}$ ($\sigma$-algebra of events)
Measure: $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ with $P(\Omega)=1$
  • $\sigma$-algebra ($\mathcal{F}$): A collection of subsets closed under complement and countable union. It defines "what can be measured" (events).
  • Random Variable ($X$): Not a variable, but a function $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$. It must be measurable: $X^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}$ for any interval $B$. This links the abstract outcome to real numbers.

3. Deriving Properties from Axioms

Using the 3 Axioms (Non-negativity, Normalization, Countable Additivity):

A. Subadditivity (Boole's Inequality)

For any events $A, B$:

$$P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B)$$

Derivation logic: $A \cup B$ can be split into disjoint parts. $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. Since $P(A \cap B) \ge 0$, the sum $P(A)+P(B)$ is an upper bound.

B. Inclusion-Exclusion Principle

The exact equality for unions. For two events:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$
